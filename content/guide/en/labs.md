# Getting to know research groups

Lab websites show who is working on a question and how their research develops over time. Pick a project and explore its paper, code, and author pages to understand the work behind the lab's name.


## Find teams through a research question

Start with a paper whose question matters to you. Follow its authors, references, related code, and subsequent work to identify groups across institutions and regions. Compare the actual tasks, available guidance, data, compute, and requirements for joining rather than institutional reputation alone.

The examples below offer starting points across research areas and regions. They are not a complete directory, ranking, or list of current vacancies. Use them to discover related work, then verify the group’s own participation requirements. For advice on reaching out, see [academic contact and collaboration](contact.md).

## Research centers and networks across regions

Research networks and institute directories can lead you beyond a short list of well-known labs. These examples offer different ways to find researchers and their published work:

- **[ELLIS sites](https://ellis.eu/research/sites)** — A European network of research units and institutes. Follow a site to its researchers, topics, and publications.
- **[Mila directory](https://mila.quebec/en/directory)** — A Canadian institute directory searchable by expertise and member type. Follow individual researchers to their affiliated universities and projects.
- **[IIT Madras RBCDSAI](https://rbcdsai.iitm.ac.in/)** — An interdisciplinary Indian research center with themes, publications, software, and datasets.
- **[C4AI](https://c4ai.inova.usp.br/)** — A Brazilian research center whose public projects include Portuguese and Indigenous-language resources, scientific applications, and AI's social impacts.
- **[Deep Learning Indaba](https://deeplearningindaba.com/)** — An African AI community with learning events and mentorship. It is a route to researchers and local communities, rather than a single laboratory.

Use these pages to locate work relevant to your question. Check each team or program's own guidance on participation and supervision.

## Vision, robotics, and embodied AI

- **[Stanford Vision and Learning Lab, SVL](https://svl.stanford.edu/)**: visual understanding and learning, including BEHAVIOR and ObjectFolder. Start with the robot task, then inspect data, perception, and evaluation.
- **[Berkeley RAIL](https://rail.eecs.berkeley.edu/)**: learning algorithms, robotics, and vision, focused on acquiring flexible behavior. Choose a project under Publications, then find its implementation under Software.
- **[Tsinghua Embodied Intelligence Lab](https://iiis.tsinghua.edu.cn/kxyj/ktzjs/qhdxjsznsys.htm)**: an official university overview of the lab. Explore its teams and projects alongside the [embodied AI materials](embodied.md).
- **[Shanghai Jiao Tong MVIG](https://www.mvig.org/)**: machine vision and intelligence, with research and application information. Follow embodied projects to individual researchers.
- **[Berkeley AI Research, BAIR](https://bair.berkeley.edu/)**: a broader community. Read researchers' explanations in the [blog archive](https://bair.berkeley.edu/blog/archive/), then find the relevant group.

## Language models and efficient computation

- **[Stanford NLP](https://nlp.stanford.edu/)**: explore research questions through papers, member profiles, and teaching materials.
- **[MIT HAN Lab](https://hanlab.mit.edu/)**: efficient AI, compression, deployment, and the interaction of algorithms and systems. Follow work such as AWQ and SmoothQuant for quantization and inference speed.
- **[THUDM / THUKEG open-source organization](https://github.com/THUDM)**: repositories leading to models, evaluation, and papers.

## Industry: original releases and technical material

[OpenAI News](https://openai.com/news/), [Anthropic Research](https://www.anthropic.com/research), [Google DeepMind publications](https://deepmind.google/research/publications/), and [Google Research](https://research.google/pubs/) let you trace a news item to the original work. For relevant topics, inspect tasks, data, evaluation, and limitations.

## Find faculty and projects beyond this list

Look through department and institute directories in places where you could realistically study or collaborate. AI research also happens in statistics, mathematics, engineering, medicine, the social sciences, and other disciplines; the relevant team may not sit in a computer science department.

1. Search for a research question or method alongside terms such as “people”, “faculty”, “research groups”, or their local-language equivalents.
2. Follow a researcher’s recent papers and project pages to understand their active questions, collaborators, and public materials.
3. Check the team’s own opportunities or admissions page. Degree structures, application cycles, funding, and eligibility vary by institution and location; a faculty profile does not necessarily mean the group has an opening.

Compare supervision, the actual work, access to resources, working language, location, and your available time. A local team, a cross-institution project, or an open-source collaboration may each offer a useful way to begin.

## After visiting a lab page

Choose a paper whose question interests you and find earlier or later work. Look at the people behind it: student pages often contain blogs, project records, or code with details the paper leaves out.

Before reaching out, organize your questions and summarize what you have tried using [contact and collaboration](contact.md). Check current eligibility, expected preparation, supervision, location or remote arrangements, time commitment, and funding. Public papers and courses can be useful for learning even when you cannot join a program.
