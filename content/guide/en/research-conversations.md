# Research advice and conversations

When reading a paper, troubleshooting training, or preparing to talk with a researcher, it can help to see how others approach the same task. The descriptions below will help you choose what to read. As you read, distinguish what an author observed from how they explain it and what they recommend. Consider the background, resources, and goals their advice assumes before applying it.

## Starting, mentoring, and communicating

- [Michael Nielsen: Why are research skills so seldom taught?](https://michaelnielsen.org/blog/why-are-research-skills-so-seldom-taught-v30/) — why students are often expected to acquire research skills on their own.
- [Jason Eisner: How to work with a professor](https://www.cs.jhu.edu/~jason/advice/how-to-work-with-a-professor.html) — communication, records, asking for help, and collaboration.
- [Stanford: Emailing faculty](https://advising.stanford.edu/current-students/advising-student-handbook/email-faculty) — writing a first email.
- [Chris Olah: Research taste](https://colah.github.io/notes/taste/) — developing research judgment.
- [Vivek: How to be good at research](https://vivekstills.substack.com/p/how-to-be-good-at-research) — a personal account of choosing problems, iterating on experiments, and building research experience. A [Chinese translation](https://www.geekjourney.dev/docs/reading/how-to-be-good-at-research) is also available.
- [S. Keshav: Research hints](https://svr-sk818-web.cl.cam.ac.uk/keshav/advice/research-hints.html) — questions, records, and making progress.
- [Andrej Karpathy: A Recipe for Training Neural Networks](https://karpathy.github.io/2019/04/25/recipe/) — training, diagnosis, and experiments.
